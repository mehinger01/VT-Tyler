ENGH 101

What a Paragraph Argues

A college paragraph is not a container for facts. It is an argument in miniature. Every paragraph should be doing one job: proving a single claim, stated in a topic sentence, using evidence the reader can actually check.

The topic sentence is not a topic. "This paragraph is about scarcity" names a topic. "Scarcity forces every buyer and seller to make trade-offs" makes a claim — something a reader could disagree with, which is exactly why it needs proof.

Everything after the topic sentence should support that one claim: an example, a piece of evidence, a explanation of why the evidence matters. If a sentence doesn't connect back to the claim, it belongs in a different paragraph or nowhere at all.

A paragraph usually ends by telling the reader what the evidence adds up to — not repeating the topic sentence, but pushing the idea one step further: so what does this claim mean for the larger argument?

FREN 101

Le Présent: Verbes en -ER

Most French verbs end in -er in their infinitive form (parler, aimer, étudier). To conjugate them in the present tense, drop -er and add the ending that matches the subject.

SubjectEndingparler
je-eje parle
tu-estu parles
il / elle / on-eil parle
nous-onsnous parlons
vous-ezvous parlez
ils / elles-entils parlent

Common greetings that pair naturally with introducing yourself using -er verbs: je m'appelle (I call myself), j'habite à (I live in), j'étudie (I study).

UNIV 100

Using Office Hours on Purpose

Office hours are not remedial help — they're scheduled, protected time a professor has already set aside to talk with students. Most go unused, not because students don't need them, but because they don't know what to bring.

A useful office hours visit starts before you arrive: one specific question, tied to something you already attempted. "I don't get chapter 3" gets a vague answer. "I tried problem 4 this way and got stuck at this step" gets a real one.

Showing up early in the semester, before there's a crisis, also does something practical: the professor now recognizes your name when it matters later, like a grading question or a recommendation request.
