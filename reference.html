BUS 103

A framework for reading a business case

"Read carefully" doesn't tell you what to look for. This does — four questions, asked in order, every time.

Situation

What's actually happening? Facts only, no judgment yet.

Complication

What's the tension or problem? What changed or went wrong?

Question

What decision is the case actually asking someone to make?

Answer

What's the recommendation, and what's the one piece of evidence that justifies it?

The habit that matters most: before writing anything, state the Question in one sentence. Most weak case responses happen because the writer answers a question adjacent to the real one.

Group project mechanics

Not general advice — three specific things to do.

Written ownership

Within the first meeting, every task gets a name next to it in a shared doc — not "we'll figure it out," an actual assignment. This alone prevents most disputes that come up later.

The two-attempt rule for an unresponsive teammate

Two documented outreach attempts — dated, in writing, text or email, not verbal — then loop in the professor. Not a punishment, the actual professional norm. Waiting past that just absorbs the cost silently.

Presenting as a team

Whoever did a section presents that section. Fastest way to expose who actually did the work, and professors notice.

Day one of every class

Syllabus week protocol

Extends the Assignment Tracker rule already in place — every assignment goes in within 24 hours of being assigned — back to the very first day.

1
The moment a syllabus posts on Blackboard, read it once straight through. No note-taking yet, just orientation.
2
Second pass: pull every graded item and its weight into a scratch list.
3
Enter every date into the Assignment Tracker the same day, not "sometime this week."
4
Note the professor's late-work policy and office hours location or format in the Notes column. These get forgotten, and they matter most exactly when something goes wrong.
